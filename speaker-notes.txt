06. Pain point — Mike
A student life.  A continuous feed. 

The interesting thing is not simply that we watch. Sometimes we do not realise why we keep watching. We obtained 402 qualified responses, predominantly from students in Hanoi. Around 69 percent were aged 18 to 20. These students face ordinary academic responsibilities while having easy access to short-form content.

Source: Report Table 4.1; ages 18–20 = 278/402

07. Pain point — Mike
Within us.  Around us. 

We divide the question into two sides. Platforms personalise recommendations and make continuation effortless. Users also bring social influences, entertainment preferences and a tendency to seek immediate relief. Our dilemma is whether continued viewing is associated with the user, the surrounding environment, or both.

Source: Report §§2.1–2.2

09. Insight — Nguyễn Đức Kiên
Five factors.  One outcome. 

We examine five potential determinants. AI refers to perceived recommendation responsiveness, not artificial intelligence-generated content. UXF concerns effortless continuation. SI captures peer influence; PEV captures enjoyment; PS captures seeking immediate digital relief. Our outcome, BCC, measures unintended and difficult-to-regulate viewing. Each hypothesis proposes a positive association with BCC.

Source: Report proposed model and Appendix A

10. Insight — Nguyễn Đức Kiên
From literature to  questions. 

The questions were developed from concepts in earlier studies and adapted to our context. For example, recommendation research informs AI, while compensatory internet use informs PS. We are not claiming to reproduce every original scale. Our instrument contains 28 items, including a seven-item BCC measure.

Source: Report Table 3.1 and Appendix A

12. Evidence — Lê Cao Thành
How we  tested it. 

Our report presents a cross-sectional quantitative survey. We used 28 substantive Likert items across six constructs. We examined descriptions, reliability and exploratory structure before testing relationships. The results shown here are five separate regressions, not one model controlling for all predictors. Optional open responses were supplementary.

Source: Report Chapters 3–4

13. Evidence — Lê Cao Thành
Where students  spend their attention. 

TikTok was the most-used platform for 61.7 percent of the sample. Almost half selected the two-to-three-hour or three-plus-hour daily short-video categories. Brainrot-specific exposure was lower: 59.7 percent selected under 15 or 15-to-30 minutes, and 11.4 percent reported none in the past seven days. These are different measures and recall periods; we do not equate all short videos with brainrot.

Source: Report Table 4.1 and daily-duration tables

14. Evidence — Lê Cao Thành
Consistency  with a caveat. 

We first checked internal consistency. SI, PEV, PS and BCC had alpha values between .789 and .872. AI was .630 and UXF was .595. We retained the reported measures, but we should not describe every scale as highly reliable. The weaker AI and UXF reliability is a limitation.

Source: Report Tables 4.8–4.13

15. Evidence — Lê Cao Thành
Exploring the  measurement structure. 

The predictor KMO was .884 and the BCC KMO was .894, with significant Bartlett tests. The reported PCA table identifies five predictor components explaining 57.895 percent of variance. BCC has one component explaining 56.839 percent. We use the table values, which differ slightly from the report narrative. This does not mean BCC has five dimensions or that factor separation is fully confirmed.

Source: Report Tables 4.14–4.17; values taken from tables

16. Evidence — Lê Cao Thành
Two associations  stand out. 

Social Influence has the largest observed correlation, .673, followed by Perceived Entertainment Value at .632. PS is .430, UXF .357, and AI .222. All five associations are positive and statistically significant. These are correlations, so we cannot conclude that one factor causes consumption or that it has the largest independent effect.

Source: Report Tables 4.18, 4.22, 4.26, 4.30 and 4.34

17. Evidence — Lê Cao Thành
Five tests.  Positive associations. 

The separate regressions support the hypothesised positive associations. Social Influence alone accounts for 45.3 percent of the variation in BCC in its simple model, followed by 40 percent for PEV. These percentages overlap and must not be added. Standardised beta equals Pearson r in these single-predictor models. This is not an adjusted ranking of determinants.

Source: Report Tables 4.19–4.38

18. Evidence — Lê Cao Thành
The social context  deserves attention. 

The pattern makes social context and entertainment motives important areas to consider. But platform factors also have positive associations, and we have not isolated independent effects. Our practical question is how to support intentional use while recognising why students watch. Chí Kiên will connect these findings to recommendations.

Source: Report §5.1

19. Solutions — Vũ Chí Kiên
For students:  make the stop intentional. 

Our recommendations combine the original literature and our findings. Qin and colleagues connect system quality with flow, while our UXF result supports considering deliberate stopping points. FOMO literature and our SI result motivate peer agreements around sharing. Entertainment research and our PEV result suggest preserving enjoyment through planned breaks. These are proposals, not interventions we tested.

Source: Qin et al. (2022); Przybylski et al. (2013); Zhang et al. (2019) · Report §5.2.1

20. Solutions — Vũ Chí Kiên
For universities:  address the trigger. 

Kardefelt-Winther's compensatory internet use model and Liu and colleagues' stress findings informed PS. Our PS–BCC correlation is .430. Universities could incorporate trigger awareness and break planning into learning-skills activities. Peer involvement can help align boundaries with actual student routines. Evaluate these proposals before scaling them.

Source: Kardefelt-Winther (2014); Liu et al. (2021) · Report §5.2.2

21. Solutions — Vũ Chí Kiên
For platforms:  give control a place. 

Boeker and Urman's audit shows that behaviour shapes recommendations. Combined with our AI result, this motivates clearer explanations and controls. Qin and colleagues' time-distortion findings and our UXF result support testing stopping cues. A successful design should help people stop when intended, rather than assume that more engagement is always better.

Source: Boeker & Urman (2022); Qin et al. (2022) · Report §5.2.3

22. Solutions — Vũ Chí Kiên
What we know.  What comes next. 

Our study is a snapshot based on self-report and a geographically concentrated sample. AI and UXF reliability also warrants caution. The next steps are broader samples, stronger measurement and simultaneous models. We did not measure clinical addiction, cognitive decline or academic performance as outcomes.

Source: Report §5.3
